## Name the situation
- **Who they are (role, not name), what you have observed, and how long it has been happening.:** A technical team member on my current cross-functional product team. Over the past several months, I have observed a recurring pattern where they provide feedback late in the product development process, often during or after formal walkthroughs, rather than during earlier review opportunities. In several cases, requirements and documentation had been shared in advance, but questions or concerns were raised only during later-stage discussions, which resulted in additional clarification meetings and rework. I have responded by sharing materials earlier, explicitly requesting review, and creating additional alignment opportunities, but the pattern has continued.

## Make a diagnosis
- **Your diagnosis, plus one sentence on why. Is your frustration with their behavior, or with a decision you made?:** Feedback. Expectations and opportunities to review the work were clear, but feedback continues to come late in the process, creating additional clarification and rework. My frustration is primarily with the repeated behavior, but I also recognize that I have not addressed the pattern directly enough through explicit feedback.

## Write your opening line
- **One next action I will take in the next two weeks is:** Schedule a 1:1 conversation to address the recurring pattern of late feedback and agree on how and when feedback should be provided going forward.
- **The first sentence of the conversation I need to have is:** In our last few requirement reviews, I shared the materials in advance, but key questions and concerns were raised during or after the walkthrough rather than during the review period, which led to additional clarification and rework for the team.

## AI role-play
- **After you step out: what did the role-play change about how you will open this conversation for real?:** The role-play showed me that opening with the impact too strongly could make the other person defensive. In the real conversation, I would start with a specific recent example, describe the behavior neutrally, and then explain its impact before asking for their perspective. This would make the conversation feel less accusatory while still addressing the pattern directly.

## Refine and complete your charter
- **What We Own. What this team owns.:** End-to-end ownership of our product stream within the broader product. We own all features within the stream, including requirements, prioritization, design and engineering coordination, delivery, release, and rollout to end users.
- **What is out of scope.:** Features and requirements owned by other product streams, enterprise-wide capabilities, and changes that do not directly belong to our stream.
- **Cross-boundary decisions that need a joint call.:** Any change within our stream that affects another product stream, shared functionality, architecture, dependencies, or the end-to-end customer experience requires alignment and a joint decision with the impacted team.
- **How We Decide. Who decides feature and scope calls.:** The Product Manager owns feature priority, requirements, and scope within the stream, with input from Engineering, Design, Architecture, and relevant stakeholders. Engineering owns technical implementation decisions within the agreed scope
- **How cross-team conflicts escalate.:** Cross-stream scope or dependency conflicts are first addressed directly by the responsible Product Managers and Engineering Leads. If unresolved, the issue is escalated with documented options, trade-offs, and delivery impact.
- **Who resolves escalations from outside the team.:** The Product Manager and Engineering Lead handle incoming issues affecting the stream. Conflicts involving priorities or commitments across multiple streams are escalated to the appropriate product and engineering leadership.

## Show and swap your team charter
- **Where does the charter leave room for interpretation that could cause a conflict?:** The biggest room for interpretation is where our product stream ownership ends and another team’s ownership begins, especially when a feature affects shared functionality or the end-to-end customer experience. The charter should define more explicitly who makes the final decision when a feature crosses stream boundaries.
